Abstract 
Engineers widely rely on simulaƟon plaƞorms like COMSOL or ANSYS to model and opƟmise 
processes. However, seƫ ng up such simulaƟons requires experƟse in deﬁning geometry, generaƟng 
meshes, establishing boundary condiƟons, and conﬁguring solvers. This research aims to simplify this 
process by enabling engineers to describe their setup in plain language, allowing a Large Language 
Model (LLM) to generate the necessary input ﬁles for their speciﬁc applicaƟon. This highly novel 
approach allows establishing a direct link between natural language and complex engineering tasks. 
Building on previous work that evaluated various LLMs for generaƟng input ﬁles across simple and 
complex geometries, this study demonstrates that small LLMs— speciﬁcally, Phi-3 Mini and Qwen-
2.5 1.5B—can be ﬁne-tuned to generate precise engineering geometries in GMSH format. Through 
Low-Rank AdaptaƟon (LoRA), we curated a dataset of 480 instrucƟon–output pairs encompassing 
simple shapes (squares, rectangles, circles, and half circles) and more complex structures (I-beams, 
cylindrical pipes, and bent pipes). The ﬁne-tuned models produced high-ﬁdelity outputs, handling 
rouƟne geometry generaƟon with minimal intervenƟon. While challenges remain with geometries 
involving combinaƟons of mulƟple bodies, this study demonstrates that ﬁne-tuned small models can 
outperform larger models like GPT-4o in specialised tasks, oﬀering a precise and resource-eﬃcient 
alternaƟve for engineering applicaƟons. 
 
1. IntroducƟon 
Engineers across industries rely heavily on simulaƟon plaƞorms like COMSOL (COMSOL AB, 2024) 
and ANSYS (ANSYS, 2024) to model and opƟmise complex processes. However, seƫ ng up these 
simulaƟons presents a signiﬁcant barrier – engineers must manually create precise input ﬁles 
deﬁning geometries, meshes, boundary condiƟons, and solver conﬁguraƟons, or rely on graphical 
user interfaces (GUIs) which are not always available especially in open-source mulƟ-physics 
simulaƟons tools. This process is Ɵme-consuming, requires specialised experƟse, and is prone to 
errors.  
The automaƟon of geometry generaƟon, in parƟcular, represents a crucial step toward making 
simulaƟon tools more accessible to a broader engineering audience, allowing them to focus more on 
interpreƟng results rather than conﬁguring simulaƟons.  In educaƟonal seƫ ngs, automaƟon helps 
lower technical barriers that oŌen discourage students from engaging with simulaƟon tools. When 
students do not have to wrestle with the intricate details of input ﬁle creaƟon and geometry setup, 
they can devote more cogniƟve resources to understanding the underlying engineering principles. 
This reducƟon in extraneous cogniƟve load is consistent with cogniƟve load theory, which posits that 
learning is opƟmised when unnecessary processing is minimised (Sweller et al., 2019).As a result, 
educators can design more eﬀecƟve instrucƟonal experiences that emphasise conceptual 
understanding and analyƟcal skills over the mechanics of simulaƟon conﬁguraƟon. 
For computaƟonal research speciﬁcally, it is observed that automaƟon of model generaƟon 
accelerated iteraƟve design processes, allowing researchers to explore larger parameter spaces and 
achieve more robust conclusions(Carta, 2020). AddiƟonally, research highlights that language-model-
driven automaƟons in engineering educaƟon remove technical gatekeeping that historically 
disadvantaged students from non-tradiƟonal backgrounds, making advanced simulaƟon techniques 
more accessible across diverse student populaƟons (Filippi and Motyl, 2024). 
Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable capabiliƟes in translaƟng natural 
language descripƟons into structured outputs, suggesƟng their potenƟal to bridge this gap between 
human intent and technical implementaƟon. Recent studies have shown promising results in 
applying LLMs to engineering tasks, from code generaƟon to computer-aided design (CAD) (Filippi 
and Motyl, 2024b; Zhong et al., 2023). These applicaƟons leverage several key concepts, including 
prompt engineering and model-driven engineering (MDE). Prompt engineering enables few-shot 
learning, allowing models to generalise eﬀecƟvely from limited examples (Gu et al., 2022; Brown et 
al., 2020), while MDE principles help bridge the gap between high-level speciﬁcaƟons and pracƟcal 
implementaƟons (Gaydamaka, 2019; Buƫ ng et al., 2020). 
However, applying general-purpose LLMs to specialised engineering tasks presents signiﬁcant 
challenges, parƟcularly when generaƟng code for specialised tools like GMSH (Geuzaine and 
Remacle, 2009), an open-source ﬁnite element mesh generator that provides a scripƟng interface for 
creaƟng complex engineering geometries (Rios et al., 2023). Our previous work revealed that 
without domain-speciﬁc training, even large LLMs struggle to consistently generate precise 
geometric shapes that adhere to engineering standards like GMSH syntax (Alexiadis and Ghiassi, 
2024, Shaﬁq et al., 2025). These models oŌen produce outputs that fail to meet the nuanced 
requirements of engineering design (Hu et al., 2021; Han et al., 2024). While some researchers have 
explored ﬁne-tuning LLMs for CAD tasks (Sanghi et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2023), the focus has primarily 
been on larger models, which require substanƟal computaƟonal resources. 
This study builds on our previous ﬁndings by invesƟgaƟng an alternaƟve approach: the systemaƟc 
ﬁne-tuning of small LLMs—speciﬁcally Phi-3 Mini and Qwen-2.5 1.5B—for engineering geometry 
generaƟon. Smaller models are criƟcal for pracƟcal deployment in engineering contexts, as they 
require signiﬁcantly fewer computaƟons resources while potenƟal oﬀering faster inference Ɵmes and 
easier integraƟon into exisƟng workﬂows. Our hypothesis is that these smaller models, when 
properly adapted using targeted datasets and training techniques, can match or exceed the 
performance of larger generalist models while being more accessible and eﬃcient. Through careful 
dataset curaƟon and specialised ﬁne-tuning methods, we aim to enhance these models' ability to 
generate precise GMSH-compliant geometries from natural language descripƟons. While our primary 
geometries involving a single body, we also examine the models' capabiliƟes in handling geometries 
involving combinaƟons of mulƟple bodies within a single prompt—a scenario that tests the limits of 
their contextual understanding. 
In this paper, we ﬁrst detail our approach to dataset creaƟon and model ﬁne-tuning, describing the 
techniques that enable small LLMs to generate precise engineering geometries. We then present 
comprehensive results that demonstrate these models' capabiliƟes across a range of geometry types, 
from simple shapes to complex structures. Our analysis explores both the successes and limitaƟons 
of small LLMs in this domain, leading to insights about their pracƟcal implementaƟon in engineering 
workﬂows. We conclude by examining the broader implicaƟons of our ﬁndings for the future of 
automated engineering design and simulaƟon setup. 
2. Previous Work 
2.1 Eﬃcient LLM AdaptaƟon Techniques 
Recent research has demonstrated that language models can be eﬀecƟvely ﬁne-tuned for specialised 
engineering tasks. Parameter-eﬃcient ﬁne-tuning methods such as Low-Rank AdaptaƟon (LoRA) (Hu 
et al., 2021) have emerged as pracƟcal approaches for adapƟng pre-trained models to domain-
speciﬁc applicaƟons without the computaƟonal burden of full ﬁne-tuning. These techniques modify 
only a small subset of model parameters while preserving most of the pre-trained weights, 
eﬀecƟvely miƟgaƟng the problem of catastrophic forgeƫ ng where models lose their general 
capabiliƟes when adapted to new tasks.  
For resource-constrained applicaƟons, DeƩmers et al. (2023) introduced QLoRA, which combines 4-
bit quanƟsaƟon with LoRA to dramaƟcally reduce memory requirements during ﬁne-tuning. This 
approach is parƟcularly relevant for our work as it enables adaptaƟon of small models like Qwen-2.5 
14B to specialised tasks such as engineering geometry generaƟon, making deployment feasible in 
typical engineering compuƟng environments.  
2.2 LLMs in Engineering ApplicaƟons 
The applicaƟon of LLMs to engineering workﬂows represents an emerging ﬁeld with signiﬁcant 
potenƟal. Alexiadis and Ghiassi (2024) demonstrated a method for integraƟng LLMs with 
geometry/mesh generaƟon soŌware and mulƟphysics solvers, allowing users to describe simulaƟons 
in natural language. Their work established the feasibility of using LLMs for engineering simulaƟon 
setup, but primarily relied on larger models through API access, which presents limitaƟons for 
widespread adopƟon in engineering pracƟce.  
Building on this, Shaﬁq et al. (2025) explored the capabiliƟes of various LLMs speciﬁcally for 
generaƟng GMSH geometry code from natural language descripƟons. Their research idenƟﬁed 
signiﬁcant gaps in the performance of even state-of-the art models when handling complex 
engineering geometries, highlighƟng the need for domain-speciﬁc adaptaƟon. Similar eﬀorts include 
FLUID-GPT (Yang et al., 2023) for parƟcle trajectory predicƟon and MyCrunchGPT (Kumar et al., 
2023) for scienƟﬁc machine learning applicaƟons, further demonstraƟng the growing interest in 
applying LLMs to specialised engineering tasks. 
2.3 CAD and Geometry GeneraƟon 
The automaƟon of CAD (Computer Aided Design) and geometry generaƟon for engineering 
simulaƟons presents unique challenges disƟnct from general CAD automaƟon. While there has been 
progress in natural language interfaces for CAD systems, such as Alrashedy et al.’s (2024) work with 
AutoCAD, these approaches typically focus on the broader design process rather than the speciﬁc 
requirements of simulaƟon-ready geometry formats like GMSH.  
Wu et al. (2021) demonstrated with DeepCAD that treaƟng CAD operaƟons as sequenƟal commands 
can enable automated geometry generaƟon, though their approach used specialised neural 
networks rather than adapƟng general-purpose LLMs. This disƟncƟon is crucial, as engineering 
simulaƟon workﬂows oŌen require both natural language understanding and precise adherence to 
domain-speciﬁc syntax, a combinaƟon that general CAD automaƟon approaches have not fully 
addressed. 
2.4 Current Challenges and Research Gaps 
While general-purpose LLMs show promise in understanding engineering concepts, they oŌen fail to 
produce the precise, syntacƟcally correct geometry deﬁniƟons required by simulaƟon tools like 
GMSH.  
Current approaches typically rely on large models that require signiﬁcant computaƟonal resources, 
limiƟng their pracƟcal deployment in rouƟne engineering workﬂows. There is a clear need for more 
eﬃcient soluƟons that maintain high performance while reducing resource requirements. 
Most exisƟng soluƟons address either geometry generaƟon or simulaƟon setup in isolaƟon, creaƟng 
a disconnect that requires manual intervenƟon. An integrated approach that can reliably generate 
simulaƟon-ready geometry code from natural language would signiﬁcantly streamline engineering 
workﬂows. 
Our work directly addresses these gaps by ﬁne-tuning small, eﬃcient LLMs speciﬁcally for 
engineering geometry generaƟon in GMSH format. By focusing on this specialised task and 
employing parameter-eﬃcient techniques, we demonstrate that properly adapted smaller models 
can provide the precision required for engineering applicaƟons while being more accessible for 
rouƟne use. 
3. Methodology 
Our research methodology followed a systemaƟc ﬁve-phase approach to evaluate and enhance LLM 
performance in engineering geometry generaƟon.  
1. Conducted baseline tesƟng of selected models to establish performance benchmarks. This 
involved evaluaƟng three small LLMs (Phi-3 Mini (Abdin et al., 2024), Qwen-2.5 1.5B, and 
Qwen-2.5 14B (Qwen Team, 2025)) and comparing their capabiliƟes with GPT-4o (Open AI, 
2024), a larger model, in generaƟng basic GMSH-compliant geometries.  
2. Developing a specialised dataset for training by creaƟng automated scripts to generate 
instrucƟon-output pairs, implemenƟng validaƟon protocols to ensure geometric accuracy 
and GMSH compliance.  
3. ImplemenƟng ﬁne-tuning procedures using LoRA adaptaƟon techniques to Phi-3 Mini and 
Qwen-2.5 1.5B, and QLoRA for the larger Qwen-2.5 14B model to manage computaƟonal 
resources. 
4. Conducted comprehensive performance validaƟon across mulƟple dimensions, tesƟng 
models on single-geometry generaƟon tasks and evaluaƟng both GMSH syntax compliance 
and geometric accuracy. 
5. Extended our invesƟgaƟon to more challenging scenarios through experiments involving 
geometries with mulƟple bodies, comparing the capabiliƟes of both ﬁne-tuned and non-
tuned models.

5. Conclusions 
Our research demonstrates that ﬁne-tuned small language models can achieve remarkable precision 
in specialised engineering tasks. The performance of Phi-3 Mini and Qwen-2.5 1.5B in generaƟng 
GMSH-compliant geometries surpassed non-ﬁne-tuned large LLMs, with both models achieving 
perfect or near-perfect scores across mulƟple evaluaƟon criteria aŌer ﬁne-tuning. This excellence in 
single-task geometry generaƟon stands in marked contrast to the behaviour of larger models like 

GPT-4o, which oŌen required mulƟple iteraƟons and error correcƟons to produce comparable 
results. 
While Qwen-2.5 14B has more parameters, it showed inferior performance compared to its smaller 
1.5B counterpart, parƟcularly in handling complex geometries. This unexpected result suggests that 
our understanding of how model scale interacts with domain-speciﬁc training remains incomplete 
and warrants further invesƟgaƟon. 
The performance paƩerns observed raise intriguing quesƟons about the nature of geometric 
understanding in language models. While the ﬁne-tuned small LLMs demonstrated impressive 
generalisaƟon to various geometric conﬁguraƟons, the disƟncƟon between genuine geometric 
comprehension and sophisƟcated paƩern replicaƟon remains unclear. However, from a pracƟcal 
engineering perspecƟve, the consistent producƟon of error-free, GMSH-compliant outputs 
represents a signiﬁcant advancement in automated design workﬂows. 
Despite their strong performance in single-geometry tasks, our research revealed important 
limitaƟons in the capabiliƟes of ﬁne-tuned small LLMs. The models' performance degraded notably 
when handling mulƟple geometries in a single prompt, oŌen resulƟng in merged instrucƟons or 
incomplete outputs.  This limitaƟon likely stems from our ﬁne-tuning dataset design, which focused 
exclusively on single-geometry examples. Without exposure to mulƟ-body scenarios during training, 
the models lacked the contextual understanding needed to properly separate and process mulƟple 
geometric instrucƟons within one prompt. This suggests that while specialised ﬁne-tuning can 
dramaƟcally improve performance on targeted tasks, the models remain constrained by the scope of 
their training data distribuƟon. 
This research demonstrates that strategic ﬁne-tuning of small language models represents a viable 
and eﬃcient approach to automaƟng specialised engineering tasks. The excepƟonal performance of 
ﬁne-tuned small LLMs in generaƟng precise, GMSH-compliant geometries suggests that this 
approach could signiﬁcantly streamline engineering workﬂows. Rather than pursuing ever-larger 
models, focusing on targeted ﬁne-tuning of smaller, more eﬃcient architectures may oﬀer a more 
pracƟcal path forward for industrial applicaƟons of AI in engineering design.
